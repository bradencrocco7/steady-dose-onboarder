# How we teach

One job: what a new tutor at Steady Dose needs to know about how we teach, on day one. This is part 2 of your first day. You don't need to remember it all today; you'll see it again in the sample session in part 4.

## The one thing Braden wishes he'd been told

When Braden started tutoring, he thought one good explanation would work for every student. It doesn't. **The principles stay the same; the explanation changes.** Nobody told him that on his first day, so it's the first thing you're told on yours.

- **The principles** are the pieces inside the four methods: each step of calc-CARE, STAT, CASE and MAPP, in the table below. They're what you teach, and they're the same for every student. A method isn't itself a principle. Its pieces are.
- **The explanation** is how you get one student to see a piece: your words, your example, your picture. It changes with the student in front of you.

Take one piece, the Analyze step of calc-CARE: set the problem up so the units cancel to the answer's unit. One student sees it when they read the unit on each number out loud. Another needs the same problem with smaller numbers first. The principle is the same both times, and only the explanation is different.

When an explanation isn't landing, don't repeat it louder or slower. Try it another way: a picture, a smaller number, a different example, or start from what they already got right.

This page exists because of it. Braden had to work it out for himself, so it's written down for you. The rest of the page is how we teach: the stance, the methods, the sheets and how a session runs.

## The stance

The stance is how you teach, and it's the same with every student. It isn't a piece of a method and you never explain it to a student, so it isn't one of the principles above.

**We teach the why.** A student who knows why a step works can rebuild it on exam day. A student who only memorized it usually forgets it by then. Every explanation gets back to the why, and the goal is to get the student thinking at that level too.

**We answer their questions to get them there.** We don't leave a student to figure it out alone and call that learning. If they're stuck, we ask a question that points at the next step, and we answer theirs. By the end of the session they should understand it. Being told to try harder doesn't count.

## The methods

These are Braden's own methods, and every sheet and session uses them. The steps inside them are the principles. Here is each one at the depth you need today. The full method sheets are in the workspace, in `curriculum/`.

| Method | For | The steps |
|---|---|---|
| **calc-CARE** | every calculation | **C**lassify: what is it asking for, in what unit, rounded how? · **A**nalyze: set it up so the units cancel to the answer's unit · **R**eview: is the answer real, or is it 10 times off? · **E**xecute: commit, once |
| **STAT** | picking a statistics test | **S**pot the outcome's data type (a count, a rank or a measurement) · **T**ally the groups and whether they're the same people or different people · **A**nalyze the shape (normal or skewed, measurements only) · **T**est name: read it off the table |
| **CASE** | clinical case questions | **C**heck the question at the bottom first · **A**ssess what rules a drug out (allergies, kidneys, liver, pregnancy) · **S**elect first-line from what's left · **E**valuate for interactions and duplicates |
| **MAPP** | learning a drug class | **M**echanism: the class anchor, plus the core mechanism of action (MOA) · **A**pply: the main uses and the classified pearls · **P**attern: the core class pattern in one view, and the help and harm coin · **P**redict: predict it in a patient, then retrieval |

MAPP has its own words. Here is what each one means:

| Step | Its parts, in plain words |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | The **class anchor** is one short, memorable line that holds the main changes the class causes in the body. For ACE inhibitors it's "Block ACE: A's drop, B rises": angiotensin II and aldosterone drop, and bradykinin rises. The **core MOA** is the chain behind it: what the drug does, what changes because of that, and what it means for the patient. |
| Apply | The **main uses** are what the class is used for, each with its "used because". The **classified pearls** are the few facts worth keeping. Each one is labeled by type (use, side effect, risk, counseling or monitoring) and says why it happens and why it matters. |
| Pattern | The **core class pattern in one view** puts the class's helpful effects and its harmful effects side by side. The **help and harm coin** is the idea behind it: the same mechanism that helps is the one that harms, so the two are taught together, as two sides of one coin. |
| Predict | **Predict in a patient** is an exam-style question about one patient, answered from the mechanism. **Retrieval** is a set of questions the student answers from memory, with the answers out of sight. |

MAPP's rule is understanding before coverage: a student learns the core pattern of a class first, and the rarer details come later.

## How the sheets build

Every student gets the same start, then each topic climbs the same ladder.

1. **At enrollment:** a formula review and a diagnostic. The diagnostic tells us where they are before session one.
2. **Teaching sheet:** teaches before it asks. Worked examples, then questions.
3. **Practice sheet:** no hints. This shows what they can do alone.
4. **Re-drill:** only the kinds of problems they missed.
5. **Extra practice:** more reps, for a topic that's close but not solid.
6. **Quiz:** no formulas given. This shows what they've kept.

Every sheet has a **key**. The key is for tutors only and is never sent to a student. Homework is due 12 hours before the session, so there's time to grade it.

## How a session runs

Sessions are 60 minutes on Google Meet. Before each one, the tutor has a **run sheet**: the parts, the minutes for each, and what to cut first if you fall behind.

- **Open (about 3 minutes).** Tell them which gaps they've closed since last time; start with what they got right. Ask one check-in question, such as whether their exam is booked.
- **Their questions come first.** If they emailed about a problem, start there, ahead of the plan.
- **Each miss, the same way:**
  1. The problem.
  2. What happened: where each number in the question went.
  3. The fix, as one short rule.
  4. What they wrote next to what it should be.
  5. A fresh problem they work out loud while you ask questions.
- **No scores.** Show what they did next to what the question asks for; don't mark it wrong.
- **Close (about 4 minutes).** Ask what's still fuzzy. Give one habit to take into the exam.
- **After.** Write the session record: each gap marked **got it**, **shaky** or **open**, and how the session compared with the run sheet. If you ran long or didn't reach a part, say so. That's normal, and the next session plans from it.
